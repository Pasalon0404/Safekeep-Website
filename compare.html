Honest comparison

SafeKeepVault vs.
Hardware Wallets

Every cold storage tool makes trade-offs. This comparison lays them out plainly so you can choose the right one for your situation.

Who is SafeKeepVault for?

  • New to self-custody — you want to keep your own keys without buying a device first or waiting for one to arrive.
  • Privacy-minded — you’d rather not have an order with your name and address tied to a Bitcoin device.
  • Already own a hardware wallet — you want a second signer, or a backup in case your device fails or is lost.
  • Building a multisig wallet — you want a 2-of-3 or larger setup without buying a device for every key.
  • Want to check everything — you want open code on ordinary hardware, with nothing you can’t inspect.

The secure chip isn’t the whole story

Hardware wallets have one real strength that SafeKeepVault doesn’t: a secure chip that keeps the key inside tamper-resistant hardware. But in 2026 each of the three biggest makers had a serious incident, and none of them involved breaking that chip:

  • Coldcard, July 2026 — a 2021 firmware bug made seeds created on the device weaker than designed, and attackers took over $100 million (Coinkite’s advisory).
  • Ledger, October 2026 — devices sold through a reseller had a hidden implant that read the recovery phrase during setup (CoinDesk).
  • Trezor, August–September 2026 — a breach at its shipping partner exposed about 80,700 customers’ names and addresses (Trezor’s statement).

SafeKeepVault makes those weak points checkable: no device is shipped, there’s no order with your name on it, the app can be rebuilt from source and compared byte for byte, and Verifiable Dice lets you check your seed on a second device. It isn’t risk-free: software has bugs too, and while it signs, your keys are in an ordinary computer’s memory (see What it doesn’t protect against).

What it protects against, and what it doesn’t

No setup eliminates all risks. The right choice depends on which risks matter most to you.

What SafeKeepVault protects against

Malware on your daily computer

Because SafeKeepVault bypasses your host OS entirely, keyloggers, clipboard hijackers, and remote access tools running on your main machine cannot see your keys or intercept your signing session.

Network-based attacks

The Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and Ethernet drivers are deleted from the system image, and a watchdog switches off any network interface that still appears. There is no network interface for an attacker to reach, regardless of what is happening on your home network.

Closed-source firmware

Every line of SafeKeepVault is open source and auditable. You are not trusting a manufacturer's word that the firmware is secure — you can read it, compile it, and verify it yourself. The app is a reproducible build: compiling a release's source gives byte-for-byte the same file, so you can check that the release is exactly the code you read.

Supply chain tampering

You flash the USB yourself from a verified image. There is no physical device shipped from a warehouse that could be intercepted or modified before it reaches you.

Blind signing

Your full-size screen displays every output destination, change address, fee and fee-rate, the total leaving your wallet, and every input being spent before you sign. Inputs are checked against your key fingerprint and your saved wallets, and you can compare each destination with the address the recipient gave you, character by character. Nothing is committed until you have reviewed and confirmed the transaction.

What SafeKeepVault does not protect against

The secure-element gap

This is the honest limit of any software-based signer, and no password closes it. To sign a transaction, SafeKeepVault must decrypt your seed into the RAM of a general-purpose computer — for that moment, your keys exist in plaintext on the CPU. A hardware wallet's secure element never does this: the private key is generated inside the chip, is never exportable, and signing happens on-die. A strong password protects your seed at rest; it cannot protect it in use. If the machine's firmware or hardware is compromised at the moment of signing, password strength is irrelevant. SafeKeepVault narrows this gap with a true air gap — there is no network path to carry off what's briefly in memory — but it cannot replicate the guarantee that the key never leaves tamper-resistant, independently certified silicon.

Physical seizure

If someone has your flash drive and your vault password, they have your keys — and because the encrypted volume can be copied and attacked offline, your password is the entire defense. There is no secure-element PIN counter to rate-limit guesses or wipe the device after repeated failures. So use a long, fully random password from a password manager, and treat the QR unlock credential as exactly as sensitive as the seed itself: a cloud photo backup or a shoulder-surfed screen is enough to leak it. A dedicated hardware wallet with a PIN-locked secure element adds physical resistance a USB drive cannot match. For users who want to remove the risk entirely, Temporary Session stores nothing on the drive at all — without the seed, a seized drive is worthless.

Hardware-level attacks

Dedicated hardware wallets with secure element chips are designed to resist side-channel attacks (power analysis, glitching), and their keys never enter recoverable memory. SafeKeepVault runs on general-purpose hardware and does not have these protections — it is also, in principle, exposed to cold-boot / RAM-remanence attacks that read key material from memory immediately after a session. In practice these attacks require sophisticated equipment and physical access at or just after signing — they are not a realistic threat for the vast majority of users, but they are a real advantage of purpose-built hardware.

A compromised host BIOS

SafeKeepVault bypasses the host OS, but a deeply compromised BIOS or UEFI firmware could theoretically intercept activity before the vault loads. This is a highly sophisticated attack vector requiring physical access to the machine and specialized expertise — well outside the threat model of virtually all individual users.

Which option is right for you?

  • SafeKeepVault — you have a laptop or desktop, want to pay nothing and check everything, want a full-size screen for checking payments, or want backup and inheritance tools and several keys in one offline session.
  • Trezor — you want an easy, plug-and-play device with open firmware. The Safe 3, 5 and 7 have a secure chip. Buy directly from Trezor.
  • Coldcard — you’re an experienced user who wants Bitcoin-only, air-gapped signing on dedicated hardware with a secure chip. If your seed was made before the 2026 fix without enough dice rolls, follow Coinkite’s advisory.
  • Ledger — you want a polished app and support for many coins and services more than Bitcoin-only tools. Buy only from Ledger directly.
  • Both — in a multisig wallet, a hardware wallet adds physical tamper resistance and SafeKeepVault adds the big screen and backup tools. A Coldcard or Trezor One can plug straight into the vault, so both signatures are collected in one sitting.
